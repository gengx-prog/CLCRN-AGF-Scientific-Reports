\title{Evaluating adaptive graph fusion for short-range weather forecasting with conditional local convolution}
\author[1,*]{Xinchen Geng}
\affil[1]{University of Southern California, Los Angeles, CA 90089, USA}
\affil[*]{Corresponding author: gengx@usc.edu}
\keywords{Weather forecasting; Adaptive graph learning; Conditional local convolution; Reproducibility}
\begin{abstract}
I evaluate an adaptive graph-fusion (AGF) input extension to the Conditional Local Convolution Recurrent Network (CLCRN) on four coarse-grid WeatherBench-derived tasks. The module gates a graph-aggregated projection against a node-wise residual projection of the same pre-CLConv input. Forty primary models (two variants, four tasks and five seeds) and six mean-fusion ablations are trained for 100 epochs in FP32. Training, validation and testing consistently include finite zero-valued targets; checkpoint selection uses validation MAE alone. Across five training seeds per variant, AGF reduces relative-humidity MAE by 2.38\% and RMSE by 3.29\% (Benjamini--Hochberg adjusted $q=0.00604$ and $0.000653$, respectively, across eight primary tests). The other six comparisons do not establish a difference at $q<0.05$. Temperature and wind have higher AGF point estimates for both metrics; cloud-cover results depend on the metric. Latitude-weighted scores, forecast-lead errors, component comparisons and sampled missing-input diagnostics provide supplementary evidence. The findings concern training-seed variability on one existing chronological split and support a task-specific humidity improvement, without establishing competitive skill against large-scale global weather models.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
Numerical weather prediction integrates physical governing equations and remains central to operational forecasting~\cite{bauer2015quiet}.
WeatherBench established a shared setting for evaluating data-driven forecasts~\cite{rasp2020weatherbench}.
The ERA5 reanalysis provides a widely used source of atmospheric training and verification fields~\cite{hersbach2020era5}.
Learning on these fields requires choices about spatial geometry, interactions between variables, and temporal evolution.
Cubed-sphere convolutional forecasting illustrates one approach to representing the globe~\cite{weyn2020improving}.
FourCastNet instead uses adaptive Fourier neural operators for high-resolution global prediction~\cite{pathak2022fourcastnet}.
Pangu-Weather develops three-dimensional neural networks for medium-range forecasts~\cite{bi2023accurate}.
GraphCast demonstrates graph-based prediction of global atmospheric states~\cite{lam2023learning}.
These studies motivate learned spatial representations, but their different resolutions, variables, and forecast horizons preclude direct comparisons with a small graph-recurrent benchmark.

Other weather models broaden the role of learning beyond a single deterministic forecasting architecture.
ClimaX investigates pretraining across weather and climate datasets~\cite{nguyen2023climax}.
Spherical Fourier neural operators incorporate the geometry of the sphere into operator learning~\cite{bonev2023spherical}.
Neural general circulation models combine a dynamical core with learned components~\cite{kochkov2024neural}.
GenCast addresses probabilistic medium-range prediction~\cite{price2025probabilistic}.
Smaller application-specific studies also address distinct needs: neural calibration of ECMWF outputs is studied by Frnda et al.~\cite{frnda2022ecmwf}.
Zanchi et al. examine local microclimate prediction from global climate data~\cite{zanchi2023microclimate}.
Guo et al. investigate monthly climate prediction with convolutional and recurrent networks~\cite{guo2024monthly}.
Alves et al. study refinement of numerical wind forecasts in space and time~\cite{alves2025spatiotemporal}.
Their tasks differ from the 12-hour setting considered here; they provide application context rather than numerical baselines.

This study builds on the Conditional Local Convolution Recurrent Network (CLCRN), introduced by Lin et al.~\cite{lin2022conditional}.
CLCRN and its Conditional Local Convolution (CLConv) operator are contributions of that prior work.
CLConv uses geographic information to construct location-dependent kernels.
The present data occupy a regular $32\times64$ latitude--longitude grid embedded on the sphere, rather than an irregular sampling grid.
Figure~\ref{fig:background} illustrates the geometric motivation; Supplementary Fig.~S1 summarizes the controlled comparison.
I investigate whether augmenting this geometry-conditioned backbone with a learned graph improves four short-range forecasting tasks under a matched training protocol.

\subsection{Spatial representations and graph forecasting}
Graph operators provide several ways to aggregate information beyond a rectangular image grid.
Chebyshev spectral filtering constructs localized graph convolutions~\cite{defferrard2016chebnet}.
The graph convolutional network of Kipf and Welling simplifies neighborhood propagation~\cite{kipf2017semi}.
GraphSAGE learns aggregators that can be applied inductively to new nodes~\cite{hamilton2017inductive}.
Graph attention assigns learned weights to neighboring features~\cite{velickovic2018graph}.
For geometric data, PointNet demonstrates permutation-invariant processing of point sets~\cite{qi2017pointnet}.
Dynamic Graph CNN constructs feature-space neighborhoods for point-cloud processing~\cite{wang2019dynamic}.
Gauge-equivariant convolution provides a different, explicitly geometric treatment of signals on manifolds~\cite{cohen2019gauge}.
These approaches distinguish representation, neighborhood selection, and geometric constraints; their point-cloud or classification results do not establish weather-forecasting skill.

Combining graph operators with temporal dynamics has been studied extensively in forecasting, as organized in the survey by Jin et al.~\cite{jin2023spatio}.
Graph convolutional recurrent networks insert graph filtering into recurrent sequence models~\cite{seo2018structured}.
DCRNN models directed spatial dependence using diffusion convolution~\cite{li2018diffusion}.
STGCN combines spatial graph convolution with temporal convolution~\cite{yu2018spatio}.
T-GCN couples graph convolution to gated recurrent updates~\cite{zhao2019tgcn}.
ASTGCN adds spatial and temporal attention to graph-convolutional forecasting~\cite{guo2019attention}.
GMAN develops a graph multi-attention architecture~\cite{zheng2020gman}.
Continuous-time graph dynamics are explored by STGODE~\cite{fang2021spatial}.
TGNN4I addresses irregular observation times and partially observed graphs~\cite{oskarsson2023temporal}.
That irregular-time setting differs from the regularly spaced hourly inputs used here, but clarifies why temporal sampling assumptions should accompany an architectural description.

\subsection{Learned dependencies and fusion}
Learning spatial relations from data is established practice.
Graph WaveNet uses an adaptive dependency matrix alongside temporal convolution~\cite{wu2019graph}.
AGCRN combines adaptive graph learning with node-specific parameterization~\cite{bai2020adaptive}.
MTGNN learns directed relations for multivariate time-series forecasting~\cite{wu2020connecting}.
Discrete graph-structure learning treats the dependency structure itself as a learned forecasting component~\cite{shang2021discrete}.
DSTAGNN models dynamic spatial--temporal dependencies in traffic networks~\cite{lan2022dstagnn}.
Graph state-space models investigate latent relational structure within probabilistic temporal dynamics~\cite{zambon2023graph}.
Spectral temporal graph networks offer a frequency-domain formulation of multivariate forecasting~\cite{cao2020spectral}.
These methods motivate evaluating the benefit of a learned topology separately from the temporal backbone.

Related work also highlights design choices beyond adjacency alone.
Scalable spatiotemporal graph networks use precomputed representations to reduce training cost~\cite{cini2023scalable}.
DiffSTG uses denoising diffusion for probabilistic graph forecasting~\cite{wen2023diffstg}.
PDFormer models propagation delays and long-range spatial dependencies~\cite{jiang2023pdformer}.
STAEformer emphasizes the role of adaptive spatiotemporal embeddings in a transformer~\cite{liu2023spatio}.
Recent traffic studies examine multi-timescale graph representations~\cite{chen2025multiscale}.
Adaptive spatiotemporal dynamic graph convolution is investigated by Zhiguo Xiao et al.~\cite{xiao2025adaptive}.
Hongbo Xiao et al. combine adaptive spatiotemporal attention with road-topology representations~\cite{xiao2025graph}.
These traffic applications supply methodological context, while the present experiments test a specific extension on meteorological data.

The adaptive-graph and fusion design here is inspired by ASTTN, which combines geographical and learned-graph representations~\cite{feng2022adaptive}.
The distinct ASTTGN architecture studies adaptive spatiotemporal transformer graph modelling for traffic measured by loop detectors~\cite{huang2023adaptive}.
The implemented AGF module in this study gates a graph-aggregated projection against a node-wise residual projection of the same pre-CLConv input.
It does not fuse a post-CLConv representation with a graph branch, and it does not implement ASTTN's Gumbel graph masking or spatial--temporal multi-head attention.
Node embeddings, learned adjacency, sparsification, and gating are therefore treated as established ingredients rather than individually novel contributions.

\subsection{Temporal modelling and the scope of this study}
Sequence architectures provide additional context for deciding what a controlled graph-fusion experiment can establish.
The transformer introduced attention-based sequence modelling~\cite{vaswani2017attention}.
Informer investigates efficient attention for long-sequence forecasting~\cite{zhou2021informer}.
Autoformer combines decomposition with an autocorrelation mechanism~\cite{wu2021autoformer}.
PatchTST uses patches to represent time series~\cite{nie2023time}.
Crossformer models dependencies across dimensions~\cite{zhang2023crossformer}.
iTransformer represents variables as tokens to learn multivariate dependencies~\cite{liu2024itransformer}.
TimesNet maps temporal variation into a two-dimensional representation~\cite{wu2023timesnet}.
N-BEATS uses basis-expansion blocks for forecasting~\cite{oreshkin2020nbeats}.
Mamba develops selective state-space sequence modelling~\cite{gu2023mamba}.
Results showing the competitiveness of simple linear forecasting models further motivate careful controls when attributing gains to architecture~\cite{zeng2023transformers}.
These models are not rerun here; comparisons among their published scores would confound datasets, horizons, and evaluation conventions.

The contribution of this study is an AGF input extension to the published CLCRN framework and a controlled empirical evaluation.
The comparison uses identical data, training schedules, and budgets for the two local variants, five training seeds, uncertainty estimates, and multiplicity correction.
Forty primary models and six mean-fusion ablations connect the reported results to selected checkpoints and figure data.
Component comparisons and diagnostic analyses accompany the task-specific findings, including negative or inconclusive results.
This scope supports a test of incremental graph fusion rather than a claim of a new forecasting architecture or competitive skill against large-scale global weather models.
The following Methods--Results--Discussion structure specifies the implementation and evaluation conventions before interpreting the numerical findings.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{Data, chronological split, and normalization}\label{sec:data}
I use the four meteorological tasks adopted by CLCRN~\cite{lin2022conditional}.
Their WeatherBench-derived targets~\cite{rasp2020weatherbench} are 2-m temperature (K),
relative humidity (percentage points), 10-m wind components ($u,v$;
m\,s$^{-1}$), and total cloud cover (fraction). The two wind components are
predicted together; the other tasks are trained separately. Hourly data
from 1 January 2010 through 31 December 2018 are supplied on a regular
$32\times64$ latitude--longitude grid ($N=2{,}048$).

Each example contains 12 hourly inputs followed by 12 hourly targets.
Windows start 24 hours apart, yielding 3,286 examples. Chronological,
unshuffled train/validation/test partitions contain 2,300/329/657 examples.
Thus successive examples do not share a 24-hour input--target window.
For each physical variable (and separately for each wind component), the
normalization mean and standard deviation are computed over the training
input arrays across examples, input times, and nodes. The same statistics
transform inputs and targets. In the frozen implementation, these statistics are computed from the original training-input arrays. Inputs and targets are then cast to float32 before in-place subtraction of the training mean and division by the training standard deviation. This float32-before-standardization order is used for training, validation and test inference and is recorded in the processing-version manifest. Forecast errors are calculated after inverse standardization.

\subsection{Backbone and implementation-aligned AGF encoder}\label{sec:architecture}
The CLConv--GRU encoder--decoder is based on the public CLCRN implementation.
CLConv uses coordinates, geodesic distance, and orientation information to
condition local convolution. These operators and the recurrent update are
retained; AGF augments the encoder input rather than replacing CLConv.
Both local variants use trainable node embeddings, whereas the upstream
reference implementation registers its node embeddings as a fixed buffer.
Thus the control is a matched local CLCRN implementation retaining the
published CLConv--GRU operators, rather than an unchanged reproduction
of every upstream implementation choice.
During training, the autoregressive decoder uses curriculum-based teacher
forcing. At evaluation, it consumes its own previous predictions.

Let $\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{bti}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}$ be the standardized input at
example $b$, time $t$, and node $i$. A linear weather projection and a learned
node embedding, each of dimension 8, form the AGF input
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}_{bti}=\phi_x(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{bti})\Vert\mathbf{e}^{\mathrm{node}}_i
\in\mathbb{R}^{16}.
\label{eq:agf_input}
\end{equation}
Source and destination embeddings $\mathbf{E}_s,\mathbf{E}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times10}$
define a directed, row-normalized adjacency,
\begin{equation}
A_{ij}=\frac{\exp(\mathbf{e}^{s\top}_i\mathbf{e}^t_j)}
{\sum_{\ell=1}^N\exp(\mathbf{e}^{s\top}_i\mathbf{e}^t_\ell)}.
\label{eq:adaptive_adjacency}
\end{equation}
This adjacency uses the same softmax of source--destination embedding
products as ASTTN (Eq.~8)~\cite{feng2022adaptive}; the graph aggregation
and fusion implementation used here is specified below.
This graph is parameter dependent but does not change with the input
weather sample. The implementation retains the self-edge and the largest
$k-1$ non-self entries, with $k=4$, then renormalizes:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{T}_k(i)=\{i\}\cup\operatorname{top}_{k-1}\{A_{ij}:j\ne i\},
\qquad
\widetilde A_{ij}=\frac{A_{ij}}{\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{T}_k(i)}A_{i\ell}}
\quad(j\in\mathcal{T}_k(i)).
\label{eq:topk_normalization}
\end{equation}
Here $\operatorname{top}_{k-1}$ returns indices. Selection does not exclude
geographically nearby nodes, so the learned edges are potentially long-range
but are not guaranteed to be nonlocal.
Aggregation, projections, and fusion follow the source-code order:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{m}_{bti}&=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{T}_k(i)}\widetilde A_{ij}\mathbf{F}_{btj},\\
\mathbf{q}_{bti}&=\mathbf{W}_m\mathbf{m}_{bti}+\mathbf{b}_m,
&\mathbf{r}_{bti}&=\mathbf{W}_r\mathbf{F}_{bti}+\mathbf{b}_r,\\
\mathbf{g}_{bti}&=\sigma\left(\mathbf{W}_g[\mathbf{r}_{bti}\Vert\mathbf{q}_{bti}]
+\mathbf{b}_g\right),\\
\mathbf{z}_{bti}&=\mathbf{g}_{bti}\odot\mathbf{q}_{bti}
+(1-\mathbf{g}_{bti})\odot\mathbf{r}_{bti}\in\mathbb{R}^{8}.
\label{eq:agf_fusion}
\end{align}
Both branches act on the same pre-CLConv input. In particular,
$\mathbf r$ is a node-wise residual linear projection, not a
geometry-conditioned CLConv representation. The encoder receives
$[\mathbf F\Vert\bar{\mathbf x}\Vert\mathbf z]$; the control receives
$[\mathbf F\Vert\bar{\mathbf x}]$. The corresponding input widths are
25 versus 17 for the scalar tasks and 26 versus 18 for wind. There is no
calendar input, periodic encoding, or multi-head attention in the primary
configuration. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} shows the corrected computation.

(a) Weather projections and node embeddings form $\mathbf F$. The AGF feature

\subsection{Matched new training and checkpoint selection}\label{sec:training}
Both models use two recurrent layers with 32 hidden units, 8-dimensional
feature and node embeddings, one CLConv propagation view, and 25 geographic
neighbours. Adam uses learning rate 0.01 and $\epsilon=0.001$, batch size 32,
gradient-norm clipping at 5, and MultiStepLR milestones $\{10,20,30,40,50\}$
with factor 0.95. Every run completes 100 epochs; early stopping is disabled.
The curriculum-learning decay parameter is 2,000. All primary and ablation
runs use FP32, with automatic mixed precision and TF32 disabled.
Training and validation MAE are evaluated after inverse standardization,
including every finite target, including physically valid zero values.
Testing uses the same zero-inclusive support. Every epoch is saved atomically;
the earliest epoch attaining the minimum full-validation MAE is selected,
without consulting the test set. Checkpoints retain optimizer, scheduler,
random-number-generator and scaler states to permit interruption recovery.
All trainable parameters are materialized before optimizer construction,
and optimizer membership and gradient coverage are checked explicitly.

The prior archive used a zero-exclusive objective. Its scores are superseded
by the newly trained results reported here. The correction therefore applies
to optimization and validation selection, rather than solely rescoring
historical checkpoints. Code is frozen before training, and per-run manifests
record source hashes, configuration, seed, precision and environment.

The training environment recorded on 5 October 2026 is Windows 11,
Python 3.13.7 and PyTorch 2.10.0 with CUDA 12.8. The device is an NVIDIA
GeForce RTX 5090 Laptop GPU (driver 610.88; 24,463 MiB reported memory).
The manifest additionally records NumPy 2.4.2, SciPy 1.17.0 and pandas 3.0.0.
These are the recorded training settings; rendering the revised figures
and compiling the manuscript do not require GPU training.


\subsection{Test metrics and replicate-level inference}\label{sec:metrics}
The primary MAE and RMSE pool all finite targets over test examples, the
12 forecast times, nodes, and output components with equal weight:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{a=1}^M|\widehat y_a-y_a|,
\qquad
\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{a=1}^M(\widehat y_a-y_a)^2}.
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{equation}
These are unweighted grid-point metrics, not area-weighted scores. I also
report a latitude-weighted sensitivity analysis, replacing the unit weights
with $w_i=\cos\phi_i$ (latitude $\phi_i$ in radians) and dividing weighted error sums by the sum of weights
over valid entries. This changes the evaluation metric only; it does not
change the training objective or checkpoint selection. For cumulative horizon $h$, errors pool steps
$1,\ldots,h$; exact-step scores use only step $h$. Persistence repeats the
last input observation and is scored by the same test implementation.

Each primary task has five independently trained control and five AGF
replicates. Reusing seed labels does not create matched random streams,
because AGF changes the parameter initialization sequence. I report means,
sample standard deviations, AGF-minus-control differences, two-sided Welch
95\% confidence intervals, and two-sided Welch tests. Benjamini--Hochberg
correction is applied across the eight task/metric tests. The 95\% intervals are marginal, not simultaneous or multiplicity-adjusted intervals; the adjusted $q$ values govern the primary significance statements. A two-sided exact
permutation test of the difference in means provides a sensitivity check
under the null of exchangeable group labels, over the
$\binom{10}{5}=252$ group allocations. This
replaces the previous paired sign-test interpretation. With only five seeds
per group, these intervals characterize training variability on one fixed
split; they do not quantify uncertainty across climates, datasets, or test
periods. A non-significant result is not evidence of equivalence. With five replicates per group, distributional assumptions are difficult to assess; the exact permutation check is also conditional on exchangeability and does not remove this small-sample limitation.

\subsection{Provenance and supplementary experiments}\label{sec:provenance}
The fixed experiment manifest contains 40 newly trained primary models:
control and AGF for four tasks and seeds 2021--2025. Six additional newly
trained mean-fusion models cover temperature and humidity with seeds
2023--2025. Every model completes the same 100-epoch FP32 protocol.
The corresponding control and gated-AGF ablation rows reuse exactly those
three seeds from the primary experiment; they are not separately selected
favourable runs. Without AGF, the entire input augmentation is removed.
Without a learned gate, the fusion is $\mathbf z=(\mathbf q+\mathbf r)/2$.

All accuracy tables, horizon plots, weighted scores and gate diagnostics
are regenerated from this common set of new checkpoints. Missing-input
diagnostics use a documented shared subset. Historical tuning trials and
short screening runs are not used as substitutes for these replicates.
Source and checkpoint hashes, seed-level CSV files, validation-selection
records and figure metadata are retained with the analysis.

The external CLCRN reference means are traceable to Table~2 of the published
AAAI paper~\cite{lin2022conditional}. Temperature and wind scores retain
their displayed units; humidity errors are multiplied by 10 and cloud-cover
errors divided by 10 to match the units used here. These historical values were not
recomputed with the present evaluator. WeatherBench's standard evaluator
uses cosine-latitude weights, whereas the released CLCRN evaluator uses
unweighted means and masks zero targets. I therefore state both conventions
explicitly without assuming that a historical reference shares the corrected
zero-inclusive protocol. The source audit and unit-conversion table are
retained in the accompanying reproducibility materials under \texttt{historical\_context}; the primary table contains only matched scores.


\subsection{Computational scope}\label{sec:cost}
The AGF implementation constructs a dense $N\times N$ similarity matrix before top-$k$ selection, so graph construction has quadratic intermediate memory despite sparse aggregation. CLConv also materializes dense kernel operators. The recorded trainable parameter counts are 39,268 for the scalar control and 80,892 for scalar AGF; wind uses 39,437 and 81,061, respectively. The inherited inference microbenchmark is identified separately as a measurement of historical weights. Its timings are not relabelled as measurements of the newly trained checkpoints.

\subsection{Primary five-seed comparison}\label{sec:primary}

The primary results use the same zero-inclusive, spatially unweighted
evaluator for all 40 newly trained primary checkpoints. Table~\ref{tab:primary}
reports the five-seed comparison. Only relative-humidity MAE and RMSE meet
the primary threshold after Benjamini--Hochberg correction across eight
tests. The remaining comparisons are inconclusive at $q<0.05$.

For relative humidity, mean MAE decreases from 4.648 to 4.537 percentage
points (2.38\%; $q=0.00604$), and mean RMSE from 7.248 to 7.010 percentage
points (3.29\%; $q=0.000653$). Temperature and wind have higher point
estimates for both metrics with AGF, while cloud-cover MAE is slightly
higher and RMSE slightly lower. The marginal 95\% intervals for all six
of these differences include zero; the experiment does not establish
either improvement or equivalence for these tasks.

\subsection{Statistical and spatial-weighting sensitivity}\label{sec:statistics}

The exact permutation analysis supports the same two relative-humidity
comparisons after separate BH correction across its eight tests
($q=0.03175$ for both MAE and RMSE). The other six adjusted values range
from 0.771 to 0.778. Welch tests remain the primary analysis; the
permutation results are a sensitivity check with the exchangeability
qualification in Methods. Supplementary Fig.~S2 displays the primary mean
differences and marginal Welch intervals on a relative scale.

Latitude weighting changes only the evaluation metric. The Supplementary
Information section ``Latitude-weighted sensitivity'' reports
cosine-latitude-weighted scores from the same new checkpoints. Relative
humidity again has lower mean MAE and RMSE (2.73\% and 3.86\%, respectively);
its adjusted values are 0.01076 and 0.000871. These eight Welch tests form
a separate sensitivity family, not additional primary discoveries.

The temperature and humidity ablations use seeds 2023--2025 for all three

\subsection{Missing-input robustness}\label{sec:robustness}

Both variants are evaluated on 64 deterministically spaced common test
windows. Node-mask ratios 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 and 0.4 use mask seeds 11, 22 and
33, shared between variants. Masks remain fixed across input hours and
signal channels; masked standardized values are replaced by zero,
corresponding to the training mean. Mask results are averaged within each
training seed before summarizing five seeds. Figure~\ref{fig:robust}
displays the AGF results as relative changes from each checkpoint's clean
score on those same 64 windows. Complete results for both variants are
retained in the accompanying analysis data and Supplementary Fig.~S5.

At 40\% masking, the mean relative MAE increases for AGF are 465.9\% for
temperature, 74.7\% for humidity, 84.7\% for wind and 37.5\% for cloud
cover. The corresponding control increases are 474.1\%, 73.0\%, 85.7\%
and 35.5\%. The descriptive ordering therefore varies across tasks.
The large temperature degradation concerns this training-mean-imputation
stress test and does not demonstrate behaviour under naturally occurring
missingness. These sampled diagnostics neither establish full-test
robustness nor provide a significance test for robustness differences.

\subsection{Full-test gate diagnostics}\label{sec:gate}

Gate statistics retain all eight scalar channels, 2,048 nodes, 12 input
hours, 657 test windows and five AGF seeds per task
(Table~\ref{tab:gate}; Fig.~\ref{fig:gate}). This yields 645,857,280 scalar
gates per task. The pooled means are 0.600 for temperature, 0.570 for
humidity, 0.508 for wind and 0.475 for cloud cover. These coefficients
weight the graph-aggregated projection in Eq.~\ref{eq:agf_fusion}; the
complement weights the residual projection of the same pre-CLConv input.
The pooled activation SD describes variation across all retained
activations, not a between-seed standard error.

The profiles in Fig.~\ref{fig:gate} use the recorded 100-bin histograms.
Their quantile markers are approximations from the binned counts; the
reported means and SDs are calculated from the full activation stream.
A coefficient near one half does not imply equal information contribution,
because branch magnitudes and directions also matter. Neither a gate
distribution nor a learned graph alone establishes a meteorological
mechanism. Supplementary Fig.~S4 provides the corresponding histogram
representation.

\subsection{Computational cost in context}\label{sec:cost_results}

Across the 46 completed runs, the sum of recorded epoch training-plus-validation durations is 56.34 GPU wall-clock hours on a serial single-GPU queue. This sum excludes initialization, final test inference, interruption/restart overhead and supplementary analysis; it is not an end-to-end campaign-duration or energy estimate.

\paragraph{Historical-weight inference microbenchmark.}

The following inherited timing evidence uses archived temperature seed-2021 weights, not the newly trained weights in the accuracy tables. It measures a fixed batch of 32 standardized windows, FP32, 12 inputs and 12 outputs, on an RTX 5090 Laptop GPU after 10 warm-up iterations and 30 synchronized repetitions. Historical control/AGF mean batch latencies were 220.53/205.60 ms (iteration SD 18.53/5.43 ms), with peak allocated memory 333.65/614.50 MiB. This sequential repeated-batch profile does not establish a speed advantage, does not measure new-weight inference, and excludes preprocessing. The historical benchmark data remain separately labelled in the supplied revision package.

\paragraph{Context relative to recent global weather models.}
The scope of CLCRN-AGF is a controlled, task-specific forecast on a
$32\times64$ grid, using 12 hourly inputs to predict the next 12 hours.
Recent global ensemble systems address a substantially broader task
(Table~\ref{tab:large_weather_context}). WeatherNext~3 reports improved
probabilistic skill over WeatherNext~2 for most upper-level targets and
surface fields in its 2024 evaluation, with some exceptions at short lead
times~\cite{rasp2026weathernext3}. FourCastNet~3 reports skill competitive
with GenCast and better than IFS-ENS in its 2020 WeatherBench~2-style
evaluation~\cite{bonev2025fourcastnet3}. These reports do not establish an
accuracy ranking against CLCRN-AGF: the verification years, initial
conditions, spatial resolutions, variables, forecast horizons, spatial
weighting and deterministic versus probabilistic scores differ. In
particular, pressure-level specific humidity is not interchangeable with
the relative-humidity target used here. A numerical head-to-head comparison would
require harmonized data and verification.

\paragraph{Training cost and practical scope.}
The reported training resources also differ substantially. WeatherNext~3
uses about 6.7 wall-clock days per model seed; its two seeds jointly
require about 4.8 TPUv4 chip-years and 8.9 TPU7x chip-years~\cite{rasp2026weathernext3}.
FourCastNet~3 reports three stages: 78~h on 1,024 H100 GPUs, 15~h on
512 A100 GPUs, and 8~h on 256 H100 GPUs~\cite{bonev2025fourcastnet3}.
These resources are reported separately from inference, and different
accelerator types are not converted into a common cost unit. The
single-member inference timings in Table~\ref{tab:large_weather_context}
are neither full-ensemble timings nor measurements on the hardware used here.
CLCRN-AGF is therefore positioned as a compact setting for retraining and
controlled methodological studies; this experiment does not establish
competitive operational forecast skill or superior speed at matched
resolution and forecast coverage.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
The evidence supports a modest relative-humidity improvement from AGF
under this fixed protocol, with lower MAE and RMSE after the eight-test
correction. It does not support a general improvement across weather
targets. Temperature and wind have higher mean errors with AGF, while
cloud-cover MAE and RMSE move in different directions. All six marginal
intervals for these tasks include zero. The larger temperature replicate
spread also prevents a claim of uniformly more stable training.

All primary and mean-fusion models are newly trained with zero-inclusive
training and validation, saved every epoch and selected by validation
alone. The reported accuracy study therefore uses a common target support
for optimization, checkpoint selection and testing. Its figures and tables
use the same selected checkpoints; the horizon plot shows the AGF subset,
and the missing-input analysis uses an explicitly identified common
64-window subset. These conventions permit consistent comparisons without
assuming that historical scores used the same objective or evaluator.
Negative and inconclusive outcomes are retained in the same tables.

The gate combines graph and residual projections of a common pre-CLConv
input. Neither its learned edges nor its scalar coefficients are identified
as meteorological teleconnections, moisture transport or causal
contribution. Full-test activation profiles summarize the fitted gates,
but their pooled spread does not establish repeatability across training
seeds or explain the humidity improvement. The component comparison is
precision matched, but only three seeds and two tasks are available for
mean fusion. Removing the entire AGF augmentation also removes its residual
branch and changes model size, so that ablation does not isolate the effect
of the adjacency. Missing-input diagnostics concern sampled windows and
training-mean imputation; the task-dependent changes do not support a
general robustness advantage. No new season-stratified experiment is
claimed.

Five independently initialized runs per variant on one chronological split
give limited precision and no uncertainty assessment across different
climate periods. The seed-level intervals do not treat correlated grid
points or forecast hours as independent experimental replicates. The fixed
test split was used in earlier development and revision work; new seeds
therefore do not constitute evaluation on a newly reserved test period.
The coarse grid, separate task targets and 12-hour horizon remain distinct
from modern multivariate medium-range forecasting. Contemporary models
require harmonized data, compute and verification before a direct accuracy
ranking is meaningful. Dense graph construction limits scaling, and the
historical-weight microbenchmark is not a runtime study of the newly
trained models.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
In this controlled experiment, AGF lowers relative-humidity MAE and RMSE
by 2.38\% and 3.29\%, respectively, with both comparisons meeting the
eight-test BH threshold. No difference is established for the other six
primary comparisons. The result is a task-specific finding about
training-seed variability on the existing chronological split, not evidence
of general improvement or equivalence across targets.
The completed 40-run primary study and six precision-matched ablations
provide a traceable evaluation of an incremental AGF input augmentation.
The claims follow the observed effects, marginal uncertainty intervals and
multiplicity-adjusted tests; they do not assert architectural priority for
the individual AGF ingredients or parity with global weather models.
